Why steepness inverts the slope percentile

All three slope metrics are calculated as shorter maturity minus longer maturity. In contango, that produces a negative number. The more negative the slope, the wider the contango spread and the steeper the curve.

That means the raw slope percentile runs in the opposite direction from intuitive steepness. A low raw slope percentile means a more negative, wider spread and therefore a steeper curve; a high raw slope percentile means a less negative, narrower spread and a flatter one.

To make the display intuitive, the report converts the raw slope percentile into a steepness percentile = 100 minus the raw percentile. There is no percentile to convert today, so the steepness row reads n/a.

This inversion is applied to all three rows in this panel and to the headline steepness tile, so there is no mixed orientation anywhere on the page: higher always means steeper and more expensive to carry.

The bands are cut on the displayed, rounded figure so that the label always describes the number on screen: shallow under 25; typical from 25 up to but not including 75; steep at 75 and above. The 75 cut is descriptive banding, not an action bar.

The value column retains the workbook's original sign convention, where negative indicates contango for these three slope metrics — the condition the ladder bleeds against.

Short tick marks above a bar are the prior session.

What sets the state

The state describes the VX1 - VX2 spread compressing toward flat and then into backwardation, as the front rises relative to the second month. That is the market paying up for near-dated protection. It is context only: no action attaches to it, and nothing on this page brings a purchase forward.

Four measures appear in this panel, but only the front spread determines the Tail state.

Front spread, (VX1 - VX2) / VX1, measures the slope between the first two futures contracts. Because both contracts are tradable futures, the measure remains comparable through the roll: it simply advances to the next adjacent pair, (VX2 - VX3) / VX2. That advance happens once the front contract is within six days of expiry, not after it expires, so the strategy curve reads the fresh front rather than letting an expiring stub distort the state. On every ordinary day the strategy curve and the raw curve are identical.

Spot lead, (VIX - VX1) / VX1, compares spot VIX with the front future. It is context only and never determines state, even outside the convergence window. In testing from 2005 to 2026, when spot lead was elevated while the VX1-VX2 curve remained dormant, the front curve transitioned to the defined Tail-onset state within the next five trading sessions only 15% of the time, against a 25% baseline. In other words, a hot spot lead did not provide useful advance warning that the futures curve was about to turn.

J2_norm, (VIX - CM30) / CM30, asks a similar question but compares spot VIX with the 30-day constant-maturity level rather than the nearest listed contract. CM30 is interpolated between the two contracts that bracket 30 days, so it is not itself tradable, but it is always exactly 30 days out, which means it never suffers expiry convergence and can be ranked consistently through time. It therefore provides the cleaner contextual reading when the spot-to-front relationship is distorted by an approaching expiration.

convexity_front, CM60 - (CM30 + CM90) / 2, measures how far the 60-day point sits above or below the straight line between its neighbours, in volatility points. Positive is the same statement as the 30-to-60 rise being larger than the 60-to-90 rise: the front of the curve rises quickly and then flattens. Negative reverses it, the front rising slowly and then steepening. It describes shape rather than direction, and carries its own percentile context.

The practical hierarchy is therefore straightforward: front spread sets the Tail state; spot lead, J2_norm and convexity_front help explain the environment around that state, but none can override it.

Why there is no gate here

There is no gate. The scheduled far-rung purchase always proceeds. This panel describes what the buy point costs; it does not defer, thin, or size it, and Section 2 does not generate a sell signal.

A conserve gate formerly stood here, deferring the purchase by one cycle when the regime was Calm, far-rung richness and steepness both sat at or above the 75th percentile, and VVIX was quiet. Measured at the real decision point, the session after each expiry, it fired once in 235 cycles from 2007. Its discrimination also sat in the middle of the outcome distribution rather than in the tail, which is the only region where a far out-of-the-money call pays.

Far-rung richness is the regime percentile of the CM120 level, shown on the bar above and in the header strip. Higher means the far rung is dearer against its own regime, so unlike the slope percentiles in the Carry panel it needs no inversion: a higher number already means a dearer buy point. The one flag this panel raises is the opposite case, when the far rung is cheap against the modern era and a fixed spend buys more of it.
