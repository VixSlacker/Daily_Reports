How to read this report

Part 3 looks at what volatility has actually done. VIX and the futures curve tell us what the market is pricing — implied volatility derived from option prices, and therefore forward-looking. Realized or historical volatility tells us what the market has delivered, based on actual market returns, and therefore backward-looking. The volatility risk premium (VRP) connects the two by measuring the gap between what was priced and what was realized.

The report answers three questions, in order:

1. WHERE IS REALIZED VOLATILITY? HV21 measures its current level over the trailing 21 trading days, roughly one calendar month.

2. HOW FAR HAS REALIZED VOLATILITY MOVED, AND IN WHICH DIRECTION? HV5 − HV21 shows magnitude: how far very recent volatility sits from the trailing-month rate, in annualized volatility points. HV5/HV21 shows the same move as a ratio, which makes direction easy to read at a glance. It only carries weight at higher readings — above 1.0 is common and says little on its own, and the tiers reflect that.

3. HOW DOES REALIZED VOLATILITY COMPARE WITH WHAT THE MARKET PRICED? VRP-21 measures the premium between VIX and HV21 at a matched horizon.

Each metric has a distinct job: HV21 is level, the spread is magnitude, the ratio is direction, and VRP-21 is pricing relative to realized volatility.

All realized-volatility measures on this page are backward-looking, not forecasts. They are also intentionally short-dated. HV21 covers approximately one trading month and is closely matched to the 30-calendar-day horizon of VIX, which is what makes VRP-21 a meaningful comparison. Realized volatility over longer windows is not ranked anywhere in this suite, so nothing here speaks to what volatility has delivered beyond the trailing month.

Percentiles provide context by comparing today’s readings with history. Up to three comparison bases are shown. FULL HISTORY begins in 1990 and places the current reading within the longest available record. MODERN ERA begins in 2020 and compares against the more recent market environment; this is the convention used across the suite, so the parts can be read consistently. SAME VIX REGIME, shown for the spread and VRP-21 only, compares today only with observations from the same VIX regime and is shown only when that regime has enough independent historical episodes — separate runs in that regime — to support the comparison.

When the percentile bases disagree, that disagreement is useful information rather than a contradiction. A reading can be unusual relative to the full historical record while being fairly typical for the current volatility regime, or vice versa.

All windows are measured in trading days, and volatility levels and spreads are expressed in annualized volatility points, the same units used for VIX.

Technical detail

HV21 is the annualized standard deviation of daily SPX log returns over the trailing 21 trading days, scaled by the square root of 252 and expressed in vol points so it is directly comparable with VIX.

The band is based on percentiles rather than fixed volatility levels. A fixed cutoff would permanently define what counts as “high,” even though volatility’s normal level changes over time. A level that was ordinary in one era may be unusual in another.

Using percentiles avoids that problem because the cutoffs adjust with the historical distribution. The band is determined by the full-history percentile, so “Elevated” means high against the entire historical record. The modern-era percentile is shown for comparison but does not determine the label.

Technical detail
The two measures

The spread and the ratio are two ways of describing the same relationship between short-term and monthly realized volatility, but they emphasize different things.

The HV5 − HV21 spread measures the absolute size of the move. It tells you how many volatility points separate the two measures. A spread of five points always means HV5 is five vol points above HV21, whether the market has been calm or highly volatile.

The spread is signed, so both positive and negative readings matter. Its full-history thresholds are P10 −7.28, P25 −3.91, P50 −0.86, P75 2.19, P90 5.67 vol points. The median is slightly below zero because, in calm markets, the 5-day measure usually runs a little below the 21-day measure. A positive spread therefore already means short-term realized volatility has moved above its recent baseline. This makes the chart’s zero line a meaningful dividing point, even though zero is not the historical median.

The HV5/HV21 ratio expresses the same move as a proportion, which makes direction easy to read. A ratio of 1.0 is the pivot: above it means HV5 is higher; below it means HV5 is lower. A neutral zone of 0.95–1.05 prevents small changes from repeatedly flipping the label. The ratio is comparable across volatility levels, but it discards scale — 1.50 is the same number whether HV5 runs 10→15 or 40→60, which is what the spread restores.

HV5 is compared with HV21 rather than with VIX because VIX measures 30-day implied volatility. Comparing it against a 5-day realized measure would mismatch the horizons. Realized-against-realized keeps the comparison like-for-like.

Percentiles conditioned on VIX regime

The conditional basis ranks today’s reading only against days in the same GMM-defined VIX regime. The regime boundaries are the fitted component crossings at 14.05, 18.99, 25.82, 35.30, and 49.69, with the upper boundary included in each regime.

An episode is an uninterrupted run of consecutive trading days in the same regime. A conditional percentile is published only when that regime has at least 30 episodes. Episodes, rather than individual days, are used to judge whether there is enough independent history: 10 Crisis episodes represent 10 separate regime events, regardless of whether those episodes lasted 20 days or 200.

Full-history episode counts are Calm 211, Normal 444, Elevated 375, Stressed 187, Extreme 55, Crisis 10. Crisis therefore does not meet the 30-episode minimum and its conditional percentile is withheld. Extreme qualifies, but with the smallest sample among the published regimes.

Meeting the sample-size threshold does not mean the conditional percentile should always carry the same decision weight. At very high volatility levels, market behavior can become increasingly path-dependent and option pricing may become more reflexive. In those conditions, where today ranks within its historical regime may be less informative than how volatility and the futures curve are evolving through time.

Applied here, the two measures behave differently. The spread is strongly affected by the volatility regime: its P90 ranges from 2.73 in Calm to 39.91 in Crisis. As a result, unconditional spread bands are effectively calibrated to Elevated conditions and lose discrimination in very calm or very volatile markets — in Crisis, nearly any reading registers as an extreme burst. That is why the spread carries a regime-conditioned percentile.

The ratio does not need one. Its P90 ranges from 1.336 in Calm to 1.574 in Extreme, and the 1.50 threshold falls between roughly the 84th and 95th percentile in all six regimes. A regime-conditioned ratio would therefore add little beyond the unconditional reading.

In the Excel workbook, valid observations are identified using the criterion ≥ −1,000,000,000 rather than simply “not blank.” This excludes the empty strings returned by rolling calculations before their windows are fully populated, preventing those placeholders from being counted as observations.

Technical detail
The band

VRP-21 = VIX − HV21. It compares 30-day implied volatility with roughly 21 trading days of realized volatility, so the horizons are closely matched. The result is expressed in volatility points.

The upper band cutoff is 2.13, the full-history 25th percentile of VRP-21. The lower cutoff is zero, but for a different reason: zero marks a genuine sign change. Once VRP turns negative, realized volatility has exceeded implied volatility. That is a structural change in the relationship, so the zero boundary should not be set by percentile.

The 2.13 upper cutoff is stable across the two main historical bases. Modern-era P25 is 1.85, versus 2.13 for full history — a difference of less than 0.3 vol points. Choosing one of those bases therefore has little practical effect on the threshold.

The ZIRP era stands out within that record. Its P25 is 1.23 and its median 3.62, against 4.59 before 2013 — that period genuinely carried a lower volatility risk premium. Those years are included in the full-history cutoff, as all years are; they are broken out here so the difference is visible rather than hidden inside a single number.

The report also shows how often VRP-21 is negative, because a percentile cannot tell you that. A percentile tells you where today ranks within the distribution; it does not tell you how often the distribution crosses below zero. These are different pieces of information: rank is position; negative frequency is how often the sign flips at all.

The share of observations with negative VRP rises sharply across regimes: Calm 6.2%, Normal 8.9%, Elevated 14.1%, Stressed 18.1%, Extreme 35.9%, Crisis 80.3%.

Percentiles conditioned on VIX regime

The conditional basis — how regimes are defined and when a conditional percentile is published — is explained under “Percentiles conditioned on VIX regime” in the acceleration section above.

The band itself should not be regime-conditioned. Conditional P25 ranges from +2.27 in Calm to −19.19 in Crisis. If the cutoff were allowed to move with the regime, a Crisis reading such as VRP = −15 could be labeled “Premium full” simply because −15 is high relative to other Crisis observations. That would be statistically correct within the Crisis distribution but economically misleading: the premium is still negative.

One caveat applies specifically to VRP-21, because VIX appears on both sides of the comparison. VRP-21 is calculated as VIX − HV21, while the VIX level also determines which regime is used for the conditional percentile. In other words, VIX both helps define the comparison group and is one of the inputs to the value being ranked.

This is true in every regime, from Calm through Crisis; it is not a special feature of high-volatility periods.

Because of this overlap, the unconditional and conditional percentiles answer different questions. The unconditional percentile asks, “Is today’s premium unusual relative to history?” The conditional percentile asks, “Given the current level of implied volatility, is today’s premium unusual?”

They are therefore complementary measures, not competing estimates of the same thing.
